# Which on-screen stimuli attract dogs' attention? Evidence notes

Generated 2026-09-21. Scope: stimuli on a screen that pull a dog's visual attention (not general cognition, not dogs-using-tech).

**Sourcing rule followed:** every source below was opened in this session. Peer-reviewed items were read either as full text (PMC open-access text via NCBI's BioC API) or as the PubMed abstract (via NCBI E-utilities — same record as the pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov URL given). Where I only saw an abstract, I say so, and I claim nothing beyond it. Numbers are copied from the page. "Not found" means I looked and did not find it, not that it doesn't exist.

Tier A = peer-reviewed / university. Tier B = journalism, company claims, trainer writing.

## 1. Sources

### S1 — Donohue, Buesing, Peterson, Ersoz, Russell, Mowat (2024). "Screen interaction behavior in companion dogs: results from a dog owner survey." *Applied Animal Behaviour Science* 270:106151. — TIER A (full text read)
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10783809/ · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38223845/ · doi:10.1016/j.applanim.2023.106151
- UW–Madison School of Veterinary Medicine (Mowat lab). Authors declare no competing interests. NIH / Research to Prevent Blindness funding (per UW news release, S14).
- **Design:** owner survey 03/2022–03/2023, n = 1,246 complete responses (89% USA); 1,077 "watchers". Optional task: owners showed 4 silent 10-second clips (dog, bird, panther, traffic), each chosen for low camera position, wide horizontal subject movement, static camera, no zoom; 672 owners did it, most often on a smartphone (43%), laptop (25%), tablet (12%).
- **Content ranking (Table 5, % of watchers):** Animals 95% → within animals: dogs 93%, wild animals 65%, cats 64%, livestock 64%, horses 63%, squirrels 56%. Humans 37% (9th most popular animal subject). "Other" 36% (within it cartoons 76%, abstract movement/screensaver 14%, moving water 10%). Ball sports 28%. Vehicles 18%. Non-ball sports 17% (within it dog sports 63%, horse events 53%).
- **Test clips (USA watchers, % rated as watching all/most, tracking all/most):** dog 63%/58%; bird 51%/45%; panther 49%/42%; traffic 38%/30%. "The most consistently attended to and tracked video was the video of a dog." Note these clips were *silent*, so conspecific preference held without sound.
- **Movement vs sound:** attracted by movement 90% (n=949) vs sound 78% (n=818); movement-only 23% vs sound-only 10% (p<0.001). 55% would watch with sound muted; 17% would not; 29% unsure. Owners commented that a familiar song, voice or jingle could start the dog watching.
- **Behaviours (Table 4):** head turn / ear prick 78%; approach screen 78%; vocalize 76%; nose-touch screen 29%; look behind/beside screen 27%; bring toys 6%; run away 5%. In free-text comments: attack/lunge 3% of coded phrases, relax/sleep 1%; positive emotion 22.1%, negative emotion (fear, anxiousness) 7.0%.
- **Duration of a typical interaction:** <1 min 22%; 1–5 min 48%; 5–20 min 19%; 20–60 min 6%; >60 min 5%. 68% interact at least daily.
- **Age:** watchers' median age 4 y (mode 1 y); dogs ≤4 y interacted more often (71% daily vs 65%, p<0.0001) and with a broader range of content (e.g. vehicles 21% vs 12%). Authors: unclear whether older-dog decline is perception, motivation or behaviour.
- **Vision:** dogs rated "excellent" vision interacted daily 71% vs 54% for good/fair/poor (p<0.0001).
- **Breed:** purebreds reported broader interest than mixed breeds (OR 1.38). Sporting (28% of purebred watchers) and herding (21%) groups over-represented relative to AKC popularity estimates. BUT within purebreds, AKC group did not significantly change *which* content categories interested dogs (p=0.88) or test-clip watching (p=0.99).
- **Authors' welfare caution (abstract):** "Because many owners reported excitement in their dogs in reaction to screen content, owners may wish to determine whether video content would be suitable for use when their dogs are left alone."
- **Limitations (stated):** self-selected sample that over-samples watchers; owner-reported, unverified; breed over-representation "requires independent verification".

### S2 — Montgomery et al. (Katz lab, Auburn University) (2025). "Characterizing TV viewing habits in companion dogs." *Scientific Reports*. — TIER A (full text read, CC BY)
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12271434/ · doi:10.1038/s41598-025-06580-y
- 650 responses → 453 TV-watching dogs; new "Dog Television Viewing Scale" (16 items: following objects off-screen, responding to visual stimuli, responding to sounds; categories dogs / household pets / other animals / humans / inanimate objects / weather noises).
- PCA gave three components: **Animal** (24.5% of variance; conspecific + heterospecific stimuli, visual and auditory together), **Follow** (21.4%), **Non-Animal** (17.8%; inanimate objects + humans). Items "did not load onto the visual and auditory components", i.e. *what* the stimulus is mattered more than whether it was seen or heard.
- Mean owner-estimated attention span 848 s (SEM 69.1) — owner estimate, large spread.
- **Temperament effects:** more *excitable* dogs scored higher on Follow (tracking objects off-screen as if real). Dogs higher in *Negative Activation* (fearfulness) scored higher on Non-Animal — "more likely to respond to the non-animal stimuli (e.g., car, doorbell)".
- **No significant effect** of age, sex, breed group, prior TV exposure or owner "teaching" on any component. (Conflicts with S1 on age/breed — see §3.)
- Same self-selection limitation (88.3% of respondents' dogs watched TV).

### S3 — Graham, Wells, Hepper (2005). "The influence of visual stimulation on the behaviour of dogs housed in a rescue shelter." *Animal Welfare* 14(2):143–148. — TIER A (abstract read)
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/animal-welfare/article/abs/influence-of-visual-stimulation-on-the-behaviour-of-dogs-housed-in-a-rescue-shelter/6E849BA04353477890AE78F4E99E06AE
- 50 shelter dogs; conditions: no TV, blank screen, moving images of conspecifics, other species, humans; 4 h/day for 5 days each.
- Dogs looked at monitors only **10.8%** of observation time. More looking at moving images than blank screen, but "their interest in all experimental conditions declined over time". Conspecific images more likely than a blank screen to bring dogs to the kennel front; conspecific and human conditions slightly (not significantly) above interspecific. All TV conditions → significantly *less* vocalisation and movement than control.
- This is the only habituation data I found: attention to the same kind of footage falls within days.

### S4 — Somppi, Törnqvist, Hänninen, Krause, Vainio (2012). "Dogs do look at images: eye tracking in canine cognition research." *Animal Cognition* 15:163–174. — TIER A (abstract read)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21861109/ · doi:10.1007/s10071-011-0442-1
- 6 dogs, eye tracking, still images (human faces, dog faces, toys, letters). "Dogs preferred the facial images of conspecifics over other categories and fixated on a familiar image longer than on novel stimuli regardless of the category." Note the direction: familiar > novel here (tiny n).

### S5 — Törnqvist, Somppi, Koskela, Krause, Vainio, Kujala (2015). "Comparison of dogs and humans in visual scanning of social interaction." *R. Soc. Open Sci.* 2:150341. — TIER A (abstract read)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26473057/ · doi:10.1098/rsos.150341
- Dogs gazed longer at actors in *social-interaction* images than non-social ones. Dogs gazed longer at human than dog social-interaction images (still images). Kennel dogs viewed images less than family dogs.

### S6 — Mongillo, Eatherington, Lõoke, Marinelli (2021). "I know a dog when I see one: dogs recognize dogs from videos." *Animal Cognition* 24:969–979. — TIER A (abstract read)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33740148/ · doi:10.1007/s10071-021-01470-y
- 32 pet dogs; video of a dog vs unfamiliar animal paired with bark vs unfamiliar vocalization. Dogs attended more to where the stimulus exited when it was a dog; bark+dog-video pairing was treated as coherent. "First demonstration that dogs can recognize other conspecifics in videos."

### S7 — Lõoke, Guérineau, Broseghini, Marinelli, Mongillo (2023). "Meowing dogs…" *Animal Cognition* 26:1335–1344. — TIER A (abstract read)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37171527/ — 64 dogs; no evidence dogs recognised cats in audio-video presentations, regardless of living with cats. Implication: video of other species may work as "moving animal", not as a recognised species.

### S8 — Völter, Karl, Huber (2020). "Dogs accurately track a moving object on a screen and anticipate its destination." *Scientific Reports* 10:19832. — TIER A (abstract read)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33199751/ — eye tracking, n=14 and 12; a Frisbee thrown back and forth: its horizontal movement "explained a substantial amount of variance of the dogs' horizontal eye movements"; dogs began looking ahead to the catcher; freezing/rewinding the video re-captured attention "particularly … for the first time".

### S9 — Lonardo, Völter, Lamm, Huber (2023). *Open Mind* 7:588–607. — TIER A (abstract read)
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37840756/ — 16 dogs, eye tracking of videos of a human vs a dog agent: "Looking times and pupil size analyses suggest that the dogs' attention was captured more by the dog agent."

### S10 — Biological-motion studies — TIER A (abstracts read)
- Eatherington et al. 2019, *Animals* 9:661, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31489919/ — 48 dogs looked longer at upright than inverted *dog* point-light displays; no bias for human displays.
- Ishikawa et al. 2018, *Anim Cogn* 21:245, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29332229/ — dogs looked longer at a frontal human point-light walker than inverted control; preference depended on the dog's sociability.
- Kovács et al. 2016, *Anim Cogn* 19:513, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26742930/ — control dogs showed spontaneous preference for biological-motion pattern; modulated by personality.

### S11 — Animacy / chasing motion — TIER A (abstracts read)
- Abdai, Uccheddu, Gácsi, Miklósi 2022, *Anim Cogn* 25:1589, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35780462/ — with plain triangles on a screen, dogs "initially preferred to look at the chasing pattern", later shifting to the independently moving shapes.
- Abdai & Miklósi 2022, *Sci Rep* 12:2370, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35149772/ — breeds selected to chase vs to retrieve did **not** differ in looking at chasing motion.
- Abdai 2025 mini-review, *Front Psychol*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39830849/ — motion cues to animacy include starting to move from rest, chasing interactions, and point-light biological motion.

### S12 — Vision basics — TIER A (abstracts read)
- Neitz, Geist, Jacobs 1989, "Color vision in the dog", *Vis Neurosci* 3:119, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2487095/ — two cone pigments, peaks about 429 nm and 555 nm; dichromatic.
- Coile, Pollitz, Smith 1989, "Behavioral determination of critical flicker fusion in dogs", *Physiol Behav* 45:1087, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2813532/ — 4 beagles; dogs "can discriminate flicker at much faster rates than has been suggested by ERG data". (Abstract gives no Hz figure; I cite none.)
- S1's introduction adds: dogs have "a higher flicker fusion frequency than humans (meaning that lower resolution screens likely appear to flicker)" yet "are attentive to movement on screens".

## 2. What the evidence says, by stimulus feature

| Feature | What was found | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Other dogs (conspecifics)** | #1 in S1 (93% of watchers; top test clip even silent); preferred in eye tracking of faces (S4); dog agent captured more attention and larger pupils than human (S9); dogs recognise dogs in video (S6); Animal component dominant in S2; conspecific footage pulled shelter dogs forward (S3). | **Strong, convergent** (2 surveys + 3 lab paradigms + shelter study) |
| **Animate movement** (something alive moving, esp. laterally) | 90% of owners name movement (S1); gaze tracks horizontal motion and anticipates (S8); preference for upright biological motion of dogs (S10); chasing patterns attract initial looking even with triangles (S11); cartoons work, authors suggest, because of rapid movement and contrast (S1). | **Strong** for "movement matters"; **weak** on parameters (no study varied speed, size or trajectory on a TV) |
| **Other animals** (wild, cats, livestock, horses, squirrels, birds) | 56–65% each in S1; bird and panther clips about 50%; but dogs may not *recognise* the species (S7). | **Moderate** (owner report + one clip test) |
| **Sound matched to content** (barks, animal calls) | 78% attracted by sound; only 17% won't watch muted (S1). About 45% "always" respond to dog noises (S15 reporting S2 supplement). Bark + dog video treated as one event (S6). S2: stimulus *type* matters more than modality. | **Moderate.** Sound is an orienting cue and helps, but is secondary to motion and is the likeliest bark trigger |
| **Social interaction in the scene** | Longer gaze at interacting pairs than non-interacting (S5). | **Moderate** (one eye-tracking study, still images) |
| **Camera at dog height, static camera, subject crossing frame** | Used as S1's clip-selection criteria on stated perceptual grounds; not itself tested. | **Weak** (expert rationale only) |
| **Novelty / change** | Interest declines over days (S3); first freeze/rewind re-captured gaze (S8); but S4 found longer fixation on *familiar* images. | **Weak–moderate, mixed** |
| **Colour/contrast tuned to blue–yellow** | Dichromacy established (S12). No behavioural evidence found that colour grading affects screen attention. | **Physiology strong; applied effect untested** |
| **High refresh rate / no flicker** | Dogs detect faster flicker than humans (S12, S1 intro). No study found comparing attention across refresh rates. Modern LCD/OLED phones and tablets don't flicker like CRTs, so this is mostly a non-issue for the app; avoid low-frame-rate video. | **Physiology moderate; applied effect untested** |
| **Humans on screen** | 37%, 9th among animal subjects (S1); in S2 humans group with inanimate objects. Exception: S5 (longer gaze at human social scenes in stills). | Moderate evidence that humans are a **weak** attractor on TV |
| **Vehicles, abstract motion, water** | Vehicles 18%; traffic clip least watched; screensaver-type motion 14% and moving water 10% of the "other" subgroup (S1). | Moderate evidence they are **weak** attractors |
| **Doorbells, car horns, squeaks** | S2: responses to non-animal stimuli (car, doorbell) go with *fearful* temperament. Found nothing on squeaky-toy sounds or high-pitched calls specifically. | **Weak**, and points to alarm rather than interest |

**Not found:** any study measuring attention vs. on-screen size of the animal, speed of motion, or erratic vs smooth trajectories on a consumer screen; any independent evaluation of DogTV; any habituation curve finer than S3's day-level decline; any controlled study of lunging/frustration at screens (only survey percentages in S1).

## 3. Ranked list of stimulus features (by strength of evidence that they attract attention)

1. **Real dogs on screen**, preferably moving and interacting, large in frame. (Strong)
2. **Animate, lateral movement** — an animal crossing or moving within a static frame; chasing between animals. (Strong that it works; details untested)
3. **Non-dog animals** — wildlife, livestock, horses, squirrels, birds, roughly equal in S1. (Moderate)
4. **Congruent natural sound** — barks/whines for dog footage, animal calls for animals. Boosts orienting but is optional for most watchers and carries the highest bark risk. (Moderate)
5. **Social interaction between the on-screen animals** (play, greeting) rather than a lone static animal. (Moderate, thin)
6. **Dog-height static camera, subject filling the frame** (Weak: rationale, not data)
7. **Novelty / rotation of content** to fight habituation. (Weak–moderate, mixed)
8. **Blue/yellow-friendly contrast; smooth high-frame-rate playback.** (Plausible from physiology; untested on attention)

Weak or counter-productive: humans talking, vehicles, abstract screensavers, water; doorbells and horns (alarm, not interest).

## 4. Is anything universal? No.

- Even the best stimulus is not universal: the silent dog clip was watched all/most of the way by 63% of *already-watching* dogs (S1); both surveys over-sample watchers and cannot say what share of all dogs watch at all. In a shelter, dogs looked at screens about 11% of the time (S3).
- **Age:** S1 — younger dogs (≤4 y) watch more often and more kinds of content; decline may be visual (older dogs have reduced retinal function) or motivational. S2 found no age effect. Treat as "probably younger > older, not settled".
- **Vision:** dogs with less than excellent owner-rated vision interact less (S1).
- **Breed:** S1 — purebreds > mixed; sporting and herding groups over-represented among watchers; but breed group did not change *which* content was preferred. S2 — no breed-group effect; Abdai & Miklósi — no chaser/retriever difference for chasing motion. So: breed may shift *how much* a dog watches, with weak evidence; no evidence it changes *what* works. (Common claims about terriers and sighthounds with prey footage are plausible but I found no data.)
- **Temperament is the best-supported moderator (S2):** excitable dogs follow/track on-screen objects as if real; fearful dogs react to doorbells, cars and humans. Sociability changes biological-motion preference (S10).
- **Individual preference is stable:** owners' stated category interest predicted the dog's response to the matching clip (all p<0.001, S1) — so per-dog learning in the app is well justified.
- **Typical engagement is short:** 70% of interactions last ≤5 min (S1). Front-loading the opener is the right instinct; expect attention to be brief and intermittent.

## 5. Welfare: "attention + calm" vs "attention by arousal"

The same content classes that top the attention ranking are the ones that produce arousal. In S1, among dogs that watch, 78% approach the screen and 76% vocalize; lunging/attacking appears in 3% of coded comments and negative emotion in 7%; "relax/sleep" in 1%. The authors explicitly advise owners to check suitability before using video with a dog left alone. Against that, S3 found TV conditions *reduced* vocalisation and movement in kennelled dogs — but those dogs mostly weren't watching, and the effect faded.

Working split for the catalog (inference from the above, not something a study tested directly):

- **Attention with calm (prefer for openers when the app scores attention × calm):** dogs walking / sniffing / trotting with no barking audio; dog-POV walks; grazing livestock and horses; birds at a feeder at modest volume; slow wildlife (deer). Conspecific content with quiet audio is the best bet: highest attention class, fewest triggers.
- **Attention via prey drive (high attention, high risk of fixating, whining, pawing, lunging):** close-up squirrels, chipmunks, mice, rabbits darting; animals chasing each other; ball/fetch POV. Excitable dogs are the ones most likely to "follow" these as real (S2).
- **Attention via alarm (avoid as openers):** barking, howling, growling audio (about 45% of TV-watching dogs always react, S15); doorbells, knocking, horns (linked to fearfulness, S2); rooster crows and sudden loud calls are plausible members of this class (no data).

Practical implications for Dog OS:
1. Open with **dogs**, but choose clips whose audio is ambient rather than barking; start the first seconds at low volume and ramp, since sound is mainly an orienting cue.
2. Keep **small-prey clips as a second-line hook** for dogs that ignore conspecific footage, flagged high arousal; let the per-dog calm score veto them.
3. Never use doorbell/knock/bark sound effects as an attention "ping".
4. Rotate openers — attention to repeated TV content decays over days (S3), and individual preferences are stable and learnable (S1).
5. Expect 1–5 minute engagement; the first 30–60 s matter most. Start playback past intros and title cards (see `startAt` in `opener-candidates.json`).
6. Prefer static, dog-height camera with the animal large in frame; avoid talking humans, vehicles, screensaver-type content as openers.

## 6. Conflicts and caveats

- S1 vs S2 disagree on age and breed effects; both are owner surveys with self-selected samples.
- S4 (familiar > novel) vs S3/S8 (novelty/decline) — different paradigms, tiny n in S4; "novelty helps" is not firmly established.
- S5 found dogs gazed longer at *human* than dog social scenes (still images), against the TV survey ranking of humans as weak. Stills in a lab are not moving video at home.
- Most lab studies use trained or restrained dogs and short clips; none measured attention to a phone-sized screen at home apart from S1's owner-rated clip task (where smartphones were the most common device, 43%).
- I could not read the full text of S3, S4–S13 (abstracts only), nor S2's supplementary tables (the 45% figure comes from press coverage, S15).
