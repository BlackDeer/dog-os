# Canine perception, cognition and behavior: established science

Verified source notes, gathered 2026-09-21. Every source below was opened during the research session (PubMed
abstracts via NCBI's API, PMC full text, publisher or university pages); nothing is cited from memory.
Labels: **[abstract]** abstract read · **[full text]** full text searched · **[secondary]** seen only as

## 1. Vision

**Established.** Dogs are dichromats (cone peaks ~429 and ~555 nm): a blue/yellow axis, red and green confused.
They use colour even when brightness cues are available. Acuity is roughly a third of ours under matched
conditions; brightness discrimination about half as good. Flicker fusion is higher than in humans, and dog
eye-tracking labs now use 120 Hz displays or recommend >80 Hz. Retinal layout varies with skull shape (§8).

**Uncertain.** "Dogs detect motion better than humans" is not supported by psychophysics: their coherent-motion
thresholds are far worse. Exact flicker-fusion figures trace back to 4 beagles in 1989. Nobody has tested
whether modern 60 Hz sample-and-hold panels flicker to a dog; "dogs see TV flicker" is an extrapolation from
CRT-era reasoning. Field-of-view figures could not be verified from a primary source.

1. Neitz, Geist & Jacobs (1989). Color vision in the dog. *Vis Neurosci* 3:119–25. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2487095/ [abstract]. Two cone pigments, ~429 and ~555 nm. n = 3.
2. Siniscalchi et al. (2017). Are dogs red–green colour blind? *R Soc Open Sci* 4:170869. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29291080/ [abstract]. On an Ishihara-style test dogs behaved like red-green-blind humans.
3. Kasparson, Badridze & Maximov (2013). Colour cues proved to be more informative for dogs than brightness. *Proc R Soc B* 280:20131356. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23864600/ [abstract]. When cues conflicted, 8 untrained dogs chose by colour.
4. Lind et al. (2017). High visual acuity revealed in dogs. *PLoS One* 12:e0188557. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29206864/ [full text]. Bright light: dogs 5.5–19.5 cpd, humans 32–44 in the same rig ("roughly a threefold distance"); dim light: 1.8–3.5 vs 5.9–9.9. n = 6, large individual variation.
5. Pretterer et al. (2004). Brightness discrimination in the dog. *J Vision* 4:241–9. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15086313/ [abstract]. Weber fractions 0.22–0.27, about 2× worse than humans. n = 3.
6. Coile, Pollitz & Smith (1989). Behavioral determination of critical flicker fusion in dogs. *Physiol Behav* 45:1087–92. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2813532/ [abstract]. Behavioural CFF far above earlier ERG estimates; abstract gives no Hz figure. n = 4 beagles.
7. Miller & Murphy (1995). Vision in dogs. *JAVMA* 207:1623–34. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7493905/ [abstract]. Classic review: worse than humans on binocular overlap, colour, accommodation, acuity; probably better in dim light, flicker fusion, field of view, "perhaps" motion. Binocular overlap ~30–60° vs ~140° in humans [secondary, via #15].
8. Byosiere et al. (2018). What do dogs see? *Psychon Bull Rev* 25:1798–1813. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29143248/ [abstract]. Best modern review; stresses how little is known relative to how much screen-based testing is done; flags monitor flicker as a gap.
9. Kanizsár et al. (2017). Dogs are not better than humans at detecting coherent motion. *Sci Rep* 7:11259. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28900293/ [full text]. Threshold 42% vs 5% in humans; 120 Hz monitors used because of dogs' CFF. n = 5. After practice (2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29943082/): 29%, still far worse.
10. Völter, Karl & Huber (2020). Dogs accurately track a moving object on a screen and anticipate its destination. *Sci Rep* 10:19832. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33199751/ [full text]. Dogs smoothly tracked a Frisbee on video and looked ahead to the catcher; a freeze or rewind grabbed attention the first time. 60 Hz LCD at 50 cm; authors cite CFF "up to 80 Hz" and recommend >80 Hz. n = 14, 12.

## 2. Dogs and screens

**Established.** Dogs look at pictures and video with no training; prefer dog faces, fixate eyes, look longer at
familiar faces and at social interaction; can sort dogs from other species on a screen; follow pointing from a
life-size projected human. In labs, dogs reliably learn nose-touch touchscreen discriminations (~265 dogs
trained across labs, very low failure rate).

**Discouraging.** Attention to passive TV is brief and habituates: shelter dogs looked 10.8% of the time and
less each day. Owners mostly report *excited* reactions (approaching, vocalising), not calm watching.
Touchscreen training takes many sessions (median 15 weekly sessions for older dogs). All published touchscreen
work uses food dispensers, infrared touch frames and targets ≥2 inches on ≥15-inch displays; there is no
peer-reviewed evidence of dogs using bare phone- or tablet-sized capacitive screens.

11. Somppi et al. (2012). Dogs do look at images. *Anim Cogn* 15:163–74. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21861109/ [abstract]. Untrained dogs fixated informative regions; preferred conspecific faces over human faces, toys, letters. n = 6.
12. Somppi et al. (2014). How dogs scan familiar and inverted faces. *Anim Cogn* 17:793–803. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24305996/ [abstract]. Preference for dog faces and the eye area; familiar faces drew more fixations. n = 31.
13. Törnqvist et al. (2015). Visual scanning of social interaction. *R Soc Open Sci* 2:150341. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26473057/ [abstract]. Longer looking at social than non-social images.
14. Autier-Dérian et al. (2013). Visual discrimination of species in dogs. *Anim Cogn* 16:637–51. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23404258/ [abstract]. All 9 dogs grouped diverse dog-breed faces as "dog" vs other species on screens.
15. Broseghini et al. (2024). Pictorial depth cues elicit the perception of tridimensionality in dogs. *Anim Cogn* 27:49. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39037605/ [full text]. Shading/perspective in 2D conveys depth. n = 32.
16. Pongrácz et al. (2003). Video-projected human images for signalling to dogs. *Ethology* 109:809–821. DOI 10.1046/j.0179-1613.2003.00923.x [abstract via Crossref]. Dogs followed a life-size projected experimenter's pointing; obeyed a projected owner about as well as in person; voice alone dropped sharply. n = 10/expt.
17. Pongrácz, Péter & Miklósi (2018). Familiarity with images affects processing of life-size projections. *QJEP* 71:1457–68. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28532292/ [abstract].
18. Kaminski et al. (2009). Dogs comprehend human communication with iconic signs. *Dev Sci* 12:831–7. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19840038/ [abstract]. Replicas worked from trial one; photographs were "less consistent".
19. Graham, Wells & Hepper (2005). Visual stimulation and the behaviour of shelter dogs. *Anim Welf* 14:143–148. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0962728600029146 [abstract]. Looked at TV 10.8% of the time, declining over days; moving images beat a blank screen; all screen conditions reduced vocalising and movement. n = 50.
20. Donohue et al. (2024). Screen interaction behavior in companion dogs: owner survey. *Appl Anim Behav Sci* 270:106151. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38223845/ [full text]. n = 1,246; 86% watch; 78% approach the screen, 76% vocalise; animal content most engaging, dogs the most popular; typical bout 1–5 min for half of dogs, few over 20 min; movement attracts more than sound (90% vs 78%); herding and sporting breeds over-represented; authors caution content may not suit home-alone use. Owner report, self-selected.
21. Montgomery, Krichbaum & Katz (2025). Characterizing TV viewing habits in companion dogs. *Sci Rep* 15:20274. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40676046/ [abstract]. n = 453; behaviour clusters by animal vs non-animal stimuli; excitability and reactivity predict it; TV can enrich or stress.
22. Hirskyj-Douglas & Read (2018). DoggyVision. *Anim Behav Cogn* 5:388–405. https://doi.org/10.26451/abc.05.04.06.2018 [abstract]. Dogs given control of a TV by proximity sensor attended but did not change how they activated it. n = 2, pilot.
23. Range et al. (2008). Visual categorization of natural stimuli by domestic dogs. *Anim Cogn* 11:339–47. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18026761/ [abstract]. First automated dog touchscreen: dogs vs landscapes, transferred to novel pictures. n = 4.
24. Wallis et al. (2017). Dog–computer interactions for mental stimulation, especially in ageing. *Proc. ACI 2017*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30283918/ [full text]. **Key methods paper.** 15-inch monitor behind an infrared frame (tolerates saliva), automatic feeder, beep on correct; optimal target 200×200 px (~2 in); ~265 dogs trained since 2006; older dogs needed a mean of 15 sessions (4–40) to reach two-choice criterion, 2 of 130 failed; 1.5% showed marked frustration when two-choice began; dogs sometimes kept working after the feeder failed; authors advise supervision at home; welfare benefit stated as a hypothesis.
25. Zeagler et al. (2014). Towards an interactive touchscreen interface for working dogs. *UIST '14*. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2642918.2647364 [secondary]. Nose touches break human touchscreen assumptions; targets ≥3.5 in.
26. Kenawell et al. (2026). A four-button computerized gaming system for dogs. *Learn Behav* 54:134–144. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41186871/ [abstract]. One dog, 11 months, 66 sessions (~21 h); "substantial training investment"; no welfare measures.

## 3. Hearing

**Established.** Upper limit ~45 kHz vs ~23 kHz in humans. In kennels, classical, soft rock, reggae and
audiobooks increase resting; heavy metal increases barking and shaking. A fixed playlist stops working by day 7
(possibly day 2); variety slows habituation. Noise sensitivity is the commonest anxiety trait (32% of 13,700
dogs). High-frequency intermittent sounds (beeps) frighten more than low continuous ones.

**Uncertain.** For pet dogs briefly separated from owners, classical music was only moderately calming and an
audiobook gave no benefit. Music composed for dogs adds nothing.

27. Heffner (1983). Hearing in large and small dogs. *Behav Neurosci* 97:310–318. DOI 10.1037/0735-7044.97.2.310 [citation only].
28. LSU Vet Med, "How well do dogs and other animals hear?" https://www.lsu.edu/vetmed/deafness/hearingrange.php [opened]. Dog ~67–45,000 Hz, human ~64–23,000 Hz; methods vary.
29. Barber et al. (2020). Hearing and auditory functioning in dogs and humans. *Comp Cogn Behav Rev* 15:45–94. https://comparative-cognition-and-behavior-reviews.org/vol15_barber_wilkinson_montealegre-z_ratcliffe_guo_mills/ [abstract page]. The authoritative review; worth reading in full.
30. Lindig, McGreevy & Crean (2020). Musical Dogs: review of auditory enrichment. *Animals* 10:127. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31941064/ [full text]. Classical calms in stressful settings; dog-specific music adds nothing; rock/metal increase barking. Summarizes Wells 2002, Kogan 2012, Brayley & Montrose 2016 [secondary].
31. Bowman et al. (2015). 'Four Seasons' in an animal rescue centre. *Physiol Behav* 143:70–82. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25708275/ [abstract]. Effect gone by day 7; habituation possibly by day 2.
32. Bowman et al. (2017). Genres of music and stress in kennelled dogs. *Physiol Behav* 171:207–215. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28093218/ [abstract]. More lying with any genre; HRV best with soft rock and reggae; effect held 5 days when genre varied; more barking right after music stopped. n = 38.
33. Kinnaird & Wells (2022). Auditory stimulation and pet dogs' reactions to owner separation. *Appl Anim Behav Sci* 254:105688. https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/the-effect-of-auditory-stimulation-on-pet-dogs-reactions-to-owner/ [abstract]. n = 82; moderate effect of classical, none of audiobook.
34. Salonen et al. (2020). Canine anxiety in 13,700 Finnish pet dogs. *Sci Rep* 10:2962. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32139728/ [abstract]. Noise sensitivity 32%.
35. Grigg et al. (2021). Stress behaviors in dogs exposed to household noises. *Front Vet Sci* 8:760845. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34820438/ [abstract]. High-frequency intermittent sounds worst; owners underestimate fear.

## 4. Olfaction (the caveat)

Smell is central to how dogs gather information and decide what to do, and a screen engages none of it. Pongrácz
2003 attributes part of the projected-vs-real gap to missing odour; the shelter TV study concludes dogs may
benefit less from visual enrichment than visually dominant species.

36. Kokocińska-Kusiak et al. (2021). Canine olfaction. *Animals* 11:2463. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34438920/ [full text].

## 5. Attention and social cognition

**Established.** Dogs follow human pointing and gaze, including from video, especially after being addressed
with eye contact. They track whether a human is watching. They discriminate human emotional expressions on a
touchscreen. Moving objects and conspecifics capture attention; anticipation and surprise are measurable on
screen. Sudden alarming 2D shapes (cat, snake silhouettes) produce alarm responses.

**Uncertain.** No validated "attention span" for screens beyond owner-reported 1–5 min bouts.

37. Hare & Tomasello (2005). Human-like social skills in dogs? *Trends Cogn Sci* 9:439–44. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16061417/ [abstract].
38. Téglás et al. (2012). Dogs' gaze following is tuned to human communicative signals. *Curr Biol* 22:209–12. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22226744/ [abstract]. On video, dogs followed a head turn more after direct gaze and address.
39. Call et al. (2003). Dogs are sensitive to the attentional state of humans. *J Comp Psychol* 117:257–63. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14498801/ [abstract].
40. Müller et al. (2015). Dogs can discriminate emotional expressions of human faces. *Curr Biol* 25:601–5. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25683806/ [abstract]. Touchscreen; dogs rewarded for touching angry faces learned slower.
41. Siniscalchi et al. (2010). Dogs turn left to emotional stimuli. *Behav Brain Res* 208:516–21. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20060016/ [abstract]. Cat/snake silhouettes alarmed; dog silhouette did not.
42. Abdai et al. (2022). Chasing perception in domestic cats and dogs. *Anim Cogn* 25:1589–97. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35780462/ [abstract]. Dogs told chasing from independent motion in plain on-screen shapes: animacy cues from simple geometry register.
43. Pelgrim et al. (2025). Dogs' real-world visual environment and attention. *Cogn Sci* 49:e70080. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40546117/ [abstract]. Head-mounted eye tracking on walks; large individual differences.
- Also: Kovács et al. (2016), *Anim Cogn* 19:513–22, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26742930/ [abstract]: dogs preferred a point-light human walker over scrambled versions. n = 39.

## 6. Learning

**Established.** Reward-based operant training works; AVSAB recommends it exclusively. A clicker is not
demonstrably better than a verbal marker or food alone. A marker that sometimes doesn't pay out does not speed
learning and shifts mood pessimistic. Dogs show positive affect when *their own action* controls the reward,
and frustration when the same reward arrives outside their control. Dogs learn perceptual category rules on
touchscreens; only about half infer by exclusion.

44. Gilchrist et al. (2021). The click is not the trick. *PeerJ* 9:e10881. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33665026/ [abstract].
45. Pfaller-Sadovsky et al. (2020). What's in a click? Meta-analysis. *Animals* 10:1757. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32998242/ [abstract].
46. Cimarelli et al. (2021). Partial rewarding during clicker training… pessimistic-like affective state. *Anim Cogn* 24:107–119. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32897444/ [abstract]. 60% vs 100% payout: same speed, worse mood.
47. McGowan et al. (2014). Positive affect and learning: the "Eureka Effect" in dogs. *Anim Cogn* 17:577–87. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24096703/ [abstract]. Yoked design, 12 beagles: control over the reward → tail wagging, excitement; same reward without control → frustration, chewing the device.
48. Rothkoff, Feng & Byosiere (2024). Dogs do not prefer to contrafreeload, but are willing. *Sci Rep* 14:1314. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38225380/ [abstract]. n = 38.
49. Aust et al. (2008). Inferential reasoning by exclusion in pigeons, dogs, and humans. *Anim Cogn* 11:587–97. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18309524/ [abstract]. 3 of 6 dogs.
50. AVSAB (2021). Position Statement on Humane Dog Training. https://avsab.org/resources/position-statements/ [index page opened].
51. Vieira de Castro et al. (2020). Does training method matter? *PLoS One* 15:e0225023. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33326450/ [abstract]. n = 92; aversive methods → more stress behaviours, cortisol, pessimism.

## 7. Welfare

Signals readable from head and body, which matter for a camera-based product:

| State | Signals | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Acute stress | very low posture; restlessness, oral behaviours, yawning, open mouth | Beerda 1998 (#52) |
| Frustration | blink, lips part, jaw drop, nose lick, ears flattened | Bremhorst 2019 (#53) |
| Positive anticipation | ears pulled toward the midline | Bremhorst 2019 (#53) |
| Training stress | lip licks, yawns, panting | Vieira de Castro 2020 (#51) |
| Touchscreen frustration | pawing the frame, heavy panting, turning away, vocalising, tongue flicks, shake-off | Wallis 2017 (#24) |
| Positive engagement | tail wagging | McGowan 2014 (#47) |

Separation-related behaviour is common (17–22% in a UK cohort). Animal boredom is biologically plausible and
chronic under-stimulation harmful.

**Thin or absent.** No peer-reviewed study of laser-pointer or on-screen-prey frustration in dogs exists; the
nearest data are a correlational *cat* survey. No veterinary body gives screen-time guidance for dogs. Evidence
that screens calm home-alone dogs is weak; owner reports point to excitement.

53. Bremhorst et al. (2019). Facial expressions during positive anticipation and frustration. *Sci Rep* 9:19312. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31848389/ [abstract]. DogFACS-coded. Follow-ups not yet read: Bremhorst 2022 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34338869/) on accuracy across contexts; Boneh-Shitrit 2022 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36585439/) on automated recognition.
54. Harvey et al. (2022). Time left alone and separation-related behaviour in UK dogs. *Animals* 12:482. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35203190/ [abstract]. n = 1,807; prevalence 22.1% → 17.2% over 2020.
55. Rehn & Keeling (2011). Time left alone at home and dog welfare. *Appl Anim Behav Sci* 129:129–135 [secondary].
56. Burn (2017). Bestial boredom. *Anim Behav* 130:141–151. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2017.06.006 [summary].
57. Hunt, Whiteside & Prankel (2022). Environmental enrichment and dog behaviour: pilot. *Animals* 12:141. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35049764/ [abstract]. Play with another dog helped most; food toys least.
58. Kogan & Grigg (2021). Laser light pointers in companion **cat** play. *Animals* 11:2178. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34438636/ [abstract]. n = 618 cats; more laser play correlated with more abnormal repetitive behaviour; proposed mechanism: an uncompleted hunting sequence. No dog data.

## 8. Individual and breed differences

**Established.** Skull shape predicts retinal layout: long-nosed dogs have a horizontal visual streak,
short-nosed dogs a strong central area. Short-headed dogs and cooperative-work breeds follow pointing better and
make eye contact faster. **Breed explains only ~9% of behavioural variance between individuals**, so per-dog
learning beats breed presets. Attention peaks in mid-life; orienting slows and looking shortens with age,
buffered by lifelong training. Older and visually impaired dogs engage less with screens.

60. McGreevy, Grassi & Harman (2004). Retinal ganglion cells and nose length in the dog. *Brain Behav Evol* 63:13–22. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14673195/ [abstract]. n = 22.
61. Gácsi et al. (2009). Selection for cooperation and attention in dogs. *Behav Brain Funct* 5:31. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19630939/ [abstract].
62. Bognár et al. (2021). Shorter headed, cooperative, younger, playful dogs form eye contact faster. *Sci Rep* 11:9293. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33927315/ [abstract]. n = 125.
63. Morrill et al. (2022). Ancestry-inclusive dog genomics challenges breed stereotypes. *Science* 376:eabk0639. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35482869/ [abstract]. 18,385 surveys, 2,155 genomes.
64. Bray et al. (2021). Early-emerging, heritable sensitivity to human communication. *Curr Biol* 31:3132–36. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34087106/ [abstract]. 375 puppies at 8 weeks.

## Looked for and not verified

- A flicker-fusion number from a primary source, or any test of whether modern 60 Hz panels flicker to dogs.
- Field of view of ~240–250°; the "20/75" acuity figure (only in a society blog summarizing Byosiere).
- Exact hearing thresholds from Heffner 1983; quantitative olfaction ratios ("10,000×", "300 million receptors"). Don't cite these.
- Laser-pointer or screen-prey frustration and compulsion **in dogs**: no peer-reviewed study. A plausible, unproven risk.
- Screen-time guidance for dogs from any veterinary body: none.
- Dogs using unmodified phone/tablet capacitive touchscreens: none peer-reviewed.
- Demonstrated welfare or cognitive benefit of screen games for pet dogs: hypotheses only.
- Validation of attention estimated from a consumer front camera: none found. Lab eye tracking needs calibration training and a chin rest.
